Dry Taps and Forced Flight in Sudan’s Infrastructure War

**By Md Obaidullah**



In early July 2026, the residents of El Obeid, the strategic capital of Sudan’s North Kordofan state, faced an immediate threat. But, it was not artillery shells. After eighteen months of partial encirclement by the Rapid Support Forces (RSF), [drone strikes and rolling blackouts](https://sudantribune.com/article/315917) severed power to the city’s main pumping stations, while tightened blockade lines choked off the diesel fuel needed to run backup generators.&#x20;



Overnight, millions of residents and internally displaced people who had sought refuge in the city were left without clean and safe drinking water. By August, the UN Office for the coordination of Humanitarian Affairs warned that [acute water shortages and relentless insecurity around El Obeid](https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/08/1168109) were driving fresh waves of civilian flight, even as relief agencies scrambled to truck two million litres of emergency water to a fraction of the besieged population.&#x20;



For over three years, international diplomacy surrounding the war between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and the RSF has fixated on key areas. These include shifting frontlines, external arms pipelines, and geopolitical competition along the Red Sea.



On the ground, however, the trajectory of the world’s largest displacement crisis is increasingly dictated by a quieter form of warfare. Both belligerents have degraded water treatment plants, electrical transformers, and irrigation canals across Khartoum, Gezira, White Nile, and Kordofan. If this collapse is being treated as the collateral damage of urban combat, it is a misreading of the logic of conflict.



In Sudan today, the destruction of hydro-infrastructure operates as a low-cost instrument of siege and demographic engineering, emptying contested cities and pushing hundreds of thousands of people northward into Egypt and westward into Chad. With the UN Security Council having granted a [two-month technical extension to the Sudan sanctions regime](https://press.un.org/en/2026/sc16470.doc.htm) on October 8, 2026, international policymakers have a narrow window before December 11 to confront water warfare as a primary driver of regional instability.

# The Energy-Water Nexus as an Instrument of Siege



The networks of modern urban water management systems depend entirely on uninterrupted electricity, chemical filtration supplies, and safe access for maintenance crews. Both the SAF and the RSF have learned that serving any single link in this chain can render a city uninhabitable far faster than street-by-street combat.&#x20;

Both the SAF and the RSF have learned that severing any single link in this chain can render a city uninhabitable far faster than street-by-street combat. Pumping stations and hydroelectric transformers sit at fixed, unarmored locations. The rise of long-range drones and heavy artillery has turned these vulnerable utilities into high-value military targets.



Data compiled by the International Committee of the Red Cross and UN agencies illustrates the scale of this pattern. Strikes on power plants, water stations, and dams across [Northern State, River Nile, Sennar, White Nile, and Gedaref](https://www.icrc.org/en/news-release/sudan-attacks-critical-civilian-infrastructure-amid-escalating-fighting) have repeatedly crippled civilian lifelines and endangered technical personnel attempting repairs. In Khartoum North, early fighting and shelling around the [Bahri thermal power and water purification complex](https://www.dabangasudan.org/en/all-news/article/rsf-accuses-saf-of-criminal-act-as-khartoum-bahri-power-station-burns), once one of the largest potable water facilities in Africa capable of supplying [300,000 cubic meters per day](https://mena-water.com/drinking-water-plant-upgrade/wtp-rehabilitation-and-upgrade/), left entire neighborhoods reliant on contaminated wells or raw water drawn directly from the Nile. Further north, the Merowe Dam, which generates 1,250 megawatts of electricity and regulates water flow for northern agriculture, has faced repeated aerial assaults, including a [drone strike that set fire to a major power transformer](https://www.dabangasudan.org/en/all-news/article/drone-strikes-hit-dam-causing-power-cuts-across-northern-sudan) in January 2025 and a fresh wave of [drone attacks intercepted by the military](https://sudantribune.com/article/309008) in January 2026.



When power transformers burn, the humanitarian fallout is immediate and quantifiable. In Rabak, located in White Nile State, a single strike on the Um Dabaker power plant shut down the municipal water system, [dropping daily water production from 33,000 cubic meters to zero](https://www.unicef.org/sudan/stories/when-water-stops-0) within hours. Within twenty days of that blackout, local health clinics recorded 2,600 cases of cholera. A similar chain reaction unfolded across Khartoum, where ruptured underground pipes and stagnant water fueled a cholera outbreak affecting 40,000 people alongside thousands of dengue fever infections.



Rather than subsiding, the epidemiological toll of broken water networks has grown more lethal as the health system disintegrates. Between May and September 2026 alone, the World Health Organization recorded [3,131 cumulative cholera cases and 264 deaths across Sudan](https://reliefweb.int/report/sudan/who-sudan-sudan-health-spotlight-september-2026), representing a staggering case fatality rate of 8 percent that far exceeds the international emergency threshold of 1 percent. While reactive oral vaccination campaigns in North and West Kordofan have reached hundreds of thousands of residents, vaccines cannot substitute for functioning pipes and chlorine supply chains when armed factions actively blockade fuel and bomb electrical substations.

# The Nile Corridor’s Invisible Displacement Engine

Understanding how infrastructure destruction translates into cross-border migration requires looking at how Sudanese households navigate risk. Families in war-affected states often endure months of airstrikes, militia extortion, and market shortages in order to protect their homes and land. The breaking point arrives when the local water grid fails and the price of vendor-trucked water exceeds household survival budgets. Once fetching water requires walking miles through active sniper or drone zones to reach untreated riverbanks, staying becomes impossible.



The destruction of municipal utilities has become the main driver of the world’s largest displacement crisis.

At its peak, this emergency forced [11.5 million displaced people](https://crisisresponse.iom.int/response/sudan-crisis-response-plan-2026) from their homes. Today, more than nine million people remain uprooted inside the country, while thirty million need aid.



Ongoing fighting in West Darfur’s Sirba locality continues to push rural communities across the western border into Chad. At the same time, urban water and power networks have collapsed across Khartoum, Gezira, and Kordofan. This infrastructure breakdown has driven a massive wave of displaced people northward along the Nile corridor toward Egypt.



Egypt now stands as the largest host country for people fleeing the conflict. Official figures indicate that [1.5 million Sudanese nationals had crossed into Egypt](https://www.ohchr.org/en/press-releases/2026/03/egypt-un-experts-raise-alarm-over-violations-against-refugees-and-migrants) by early 2026. By September 2026, more than [1.08 million refugees and asylum seekers were formally registered with UNHCR in Egypt](https://reliefweb.int/report/egypt/unhcr-egypt-factsheet-september-2026), with Sudanese nationals making up over three-quarters of the total registered population. Every working day, [between 3,000 and 4,000 people approach UNHCR reception centers](https://www.unhcr.org/sites/default/files/docs/default/2026-07/unhcr-egypt-fact-sheet-june-2026.pdf) in Greater Cairo seeking legal protection, residency regularization, and basic health services.



The speed and scale of this influx have strained southern Egyptian transit hubs like Aswan and reshaped Cairo’s domestic migration governance. Having enacted its first domestic asylum law in December 2024, the Egyptian government issued the law’s [implementing bylaws in May 2026](https://reliefweb.int/report/egypt/unhcr-egypt-factsheet-september-2026) to transition refugee management from UN administration to state institutions. Yet legal restructuring in Cairo cannot absorb an endless southward pressure wave if the infrastructural drivers of flight inside Sudan remain unchecked.&#x20;



When a drone strike disables a transformer in Merowe or a fuel blockade cuts off El Obeid, the ripple effects reach desert crossings into southern Egypt weeks later. At these irregular border points, exhausted families must endure predatory smuggling networks, dehydration, and the threat of detention and deportation.

# A Policy Roadmap Before the December UN Deadline

Breaking the link between infrastructure warfare and forced displacement requires moving beyond generic appeals for international humanitarian law compliance. Policymakers at the United Nations, donor governments, and regional states must integrate infrastructure protection and decentralized resource governance directly into their diplomatic and aid strategies before the end of 2026.



First, the UN Security Council must use its upcoming December mandate review to impose targeted accountability for attacks on water and power networks. On October 8, 2026, the Council unanimously adopted [Resolution 2830](https://press.un.org/en/2026/sc16470.doc.htm), extending the Resolution 1591 Sudan sanctions regime until December 11, 2026, and the mandate of the Panel of Experts until January 11, 2027. The brief two-month technical rollover reflected sharp divisions among Council members over whether to expand or wind down existing measures. During negotiations over the next six weeks, Council members pushing for stronger civilian protection should insist on explicitly adding the deliberate targeting of water treatment plants, dams, and electrical substations, as well as the blockade of water-treatment chemicals and generator fuel, to the designation criteria for travel bans and asset freezes. Instructing the Panel of Experts to map the command responsibility and drone supply chains behind strikes on facilities like the Merowe Dam and Um Dabaker power plant would raise the political and financial cost of hydro-warfare for both the SAF and the RSF.



Second, international donors must restructure how water, sanitation, and hygiene funding is deployed inside Sudan. As of late summer 2026, the nearly $3 billion UN humanitarian response appeal for Sudan remained only [about 40 percent funded](https://news.un.org/en/story/2026/08/1168109), already forcing the closure of health facilities serving tens of thousands of displaced people in Central Darfur. Within these tight fiscal constraints, pouring donor money into repairing centralized electrical grids that can be disabled by a single cheap drone is unsustainable. Donors should instead prioritize funding for decentralized, solar-powered micro-grids at the neighborhood and village levels, which eliminate dependence on blockaded diesel supply lines and single-point power transformers. Backing [UNICEF’s nascent efforts to support local Sudanese production of water treatment chemicals](https://www.unicef.org/sudan/stories/when-water-stops-0) can simultaneously bypass cross-line siege bottlenecks and sustain local livelihoods.



Finally, regional diplomacy led by Egypt, the African Union, and international envoys must link border management to internal service stabilization. As Egypt operationalizes its May 2026 asylum bylaws with [UNHCR requesting $133.5 million for its 2026 Egypt response](https://www.unhcr.org/sites/default/files/docs/default/2026-07/unhcr-egypt-fact-sheet-june-2026.pdf), European and Gulf donors should pair financial support for Egyptian host municipalities in Aswan, Alexandria, and Cairo with protected cross-border humanitarian corridors into Northern State and Kordofan. Establishing negotiated "infrastructure deconfliction zones" around major pumping stations along the Nile and in besieged hubs like El Obeid offers a pragmatic starting point for ceasefire talks. In a war where thirst has become as deadly as artillery, protecting the pipes, pumps, and technicians that keep Sudan’s water flowing is the most immediate way to save lives and stem a regional displacement catastrophe.
