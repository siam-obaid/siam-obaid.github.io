How to Write a Personal Statement or Personal History Statement

A practical guide to telling your story, choosing formative experiences, and connecting your background to graduate study.

Md. Obaidullah · November 8, 2026

Post viewsLoading…

If the statement of purpose is about your research, the personal statement is about you. In roughly 500–1,000 words, depending on the program’s limit, you describe the path that brought you to graduate school, what shaped it, and what you’ll bring to the program. Many PhD applications ask for both, and committees read them side by side.

That pairing is where many applicants go wrong. Some write a second statement of purpose and repeat their research plans. Others write an emotional essay about hardship that never connects to graduate study.

A good personal statement tells a specific story about your path and shows what you did with your experiences. Then it links them to the scholar you’re becoming.

This post is a companion to my guide to the statement of purpose. It’s written with political science PhD applicants in mind, but most of it applies to any field.

First, check what the program means

Programs use the same names for different documents, so read the prompt before you write a word.

  • At some programs, “personal statement” means the statement of purpose. Yale’s political science department, for example, uses the term for a statement about your research motivation, experience, plans, and fit.
  • At others, it’s a separate essay about your background. Cornell’s Graduate School requires both an academic statement of purpose and a personal statement for every graduate degree program, and Harvard’s graduate school asks for a personal statement in some programs.
  • “Personal history statement” is Berkeley’s term, though other schools use it too. At Berkeley it’s required alongside the statement of purpose, and its prompt asks how your background and life experiences have influenced your decision to pursue a graduate degree.
  • Some schools use other labels, such as “diversity statement.”

How it differs from the statement of purpose

The two essays answer different questions.

What each statement needs to do
AspectStatement of purposePersonal statement
Main questionWhat will you study, and are you ready?What path brought you here, and what will you bring?
FocusResearch questions, preparation, fit with facultyBackground, formative experiences, choices, contributions
EvidenceResearch experience, methods training, a thesisResponsibilities, obstacles, teaching, service, leadership
ToneAcademic and analyticalPersonal, but still professional
Typical failureA passion essay with no research questionA second statement of purpose, or a hardship story with no point

Both Harvard and Berkeley tell applicants not to repeat the statement of purpose in the personal statement. If a paragraph could sit in either essay, it belongs in only one of them.

What committees look for

Read enough prompts and the same four requests keep coming up.

  1. What shaped your path? Berkeley, Cornell, and UC Davis all ask how your background and experiences influenced your decision to pursue a graduate degree.
  2. What did you do with what you had? Purdue’s political science department has told applicants that reviewers want to understand “your accomplishments relative to your opportunities.” A 3.6 GPA earned while working full-time reads differently once the committee knows about the job.
  3. Are you ready for a long program? Purdue has asked how your experiences make you persistent and resourceful, and Cornell asks about your readiness for graduate study.
  4. What will you add? Harvard asks how your experiences will contribute to the academic environment or community in your program. Think of yourself as a future colleague, teacher, mentor, and organizer.

Choosing what to write about

You don’t need a dramatic or deeply emotional story. You need one or two experiences that shaped your path and that you can connect to graduate study.

Good raw material includes:

  • How you came to your questions: an experience that changed what you cared about.
  • Responsibilities you carried while studying, such as paid work, family obligations, or caregiving.
  • Being the first in your family to attend university, as I am, or learning an unfamiliar academic system.
  • Teaching, tutoring, mentoring, or organizing.
  • Work outside academia that shaped your thinking, in government, NGOs, journalism, the military, or business.
  • Moving between countries, languages, or institutions.

Test each candidate story with one question: what does this have to do with the scholar you want to become? If you can’t answer in a sentence, pick another.

A structure that works

Most strong personal statements follow a simple arc:

  1. A specific moment or setting. Open with concrete details, not a summary of your life.
  2. What you did. Describe your choices, what you learned, and how your direction changed.
  3. The thread to graduate school. Show how these experiences shaped your interests, your approach to research, or your commitment to teaching and mentoring.
  4. What you’ll contribute. Name concrete ways you’ll take part in the department.

With a 500-word limit, each part gets a short paragraph. With more room, give the middle two more space. Limits vary, so check each one.

A weak and a stronger paragraph

A weak example:

Throughout my life, I have faced many challenges, but I have never given up. These experiences taught me resilience and made me passionate about helping others. As someone from a diverse background, I will bring a unique perspective to your program.

Every sentence claims a quality, and none of them shows it. “A unique perspective” is especially empty, because the committee can’t tell what the perspective is.

A stronger illustrative example:

For three years before graduate school, I worked as a caseworker at a legal aid office, helping tenants respond to eviction notices. Many of my clients missed their hearings because the notices arrived late, only in English, and with no explanation of the process. I began translating notices and calling clients the week before their court dates, and fewer of them missed hearings. That work is why I study how the design of public institutions shapes who can use them. It’s also why I want to teach: as a TA, I’d like to help first-generation undergraduates learn how these institutions work.

The second paragraph is specific about the setting, shows what the writer did, and connects the experience to both research and contribution. It does all that without a single adjective about the writer’s character.

Writing about identity, hardship, and difficult experiences

You decide what to share. Harvard’s graduate school says this directly: applicants don’t need to share sensitive information and should include only what they’re comfortable disclosing. The same is true at any program.

  • Write about experiences and actions, not just labels. “As a first-generation student” names a category. Working twenty hours a week, teaching yourself statistics, or starting a study group tells the committee about you.
  • If you write about hardship, keep the weight on what you did and where you’re headed. Berkeley’s guidance asks applicants to write “affirmatively,” showing perseverance despite obstacles.
  • You don’t need hardship at all. Committees aren’t ranking adversity, and formative work, teaching, and responsibilities make good material too.
  • Avoid comparing your difficulties with anyone else’s, and avoid casting communities you’ve worked with as people who needed rescuing.

For international applicants

If you’re applying from outside the US, a few points matter more.

  • Use the essay to explain your path: why a US PhD, what you did before, whether work, teaching, or research, and how you got here.
  • Give context for your record without making excuses. If research opportunities were scarce where you studied, say what you did with what was available. That’s what judging accomplishments against opportunities looks like in practice.
  • Skip the travel-guide paragraph. The committee needs your story, not a description of your country.
  • Don’t treat your nationality as your contribution. Being from Bangladesh, Nigeria, or Peru is a fact about you. What you’ve done, and what you’ll bring to the department, is the content.

Common mistakes

  • Repeating your statement of purpose. The committee reads both essays together, so overlap wastes your space.
  • A list of hardships with no reflection or action.
  • Claiming qualities instead of showing them: “resilient,” “passionate,” “hardworking.”
  • Vague claims about diversity or “a unique perspective” with nothing specific behind them.
  • The savior story, where a community you worked with needed rescuing and you were the hero.
  • Oversharing details you’d later regret, or that unsettle the reader without adding anything.
  • Blaming other people for setbacks.
  • Ignoring the prompt or the length limit.

Drafting and revising

  1. Write your statement of purpose first. The personal statement can then cover what the statement of purpose can’t.
  2. Brainstorm before you draft. List five or six formative experiences, then pick one or two.
  3. Read the two statements side by side and cut anything that appears in both.
  4. Get feedback from someone who knows you well and from someone who doesn’t. The first checks accuracy; the second checks whether a stranger can follow the story.
  5. Keep the tone professional. The MIT Communication Lab recommends preserving some formality even though the content is personal.
  6. Read it aloud.

The AI rules from my statement of purpose post apply here too. Cornell’s AI policy, for example, covers personal statements as well as statements of purpose. This is also the essay where AI drafting hurts most, since its whole job is to sound like you.

A final checklist

Before you submit, make sure that:

  • The essay answers this program’s current prompt.
  • It tells one or two specific stories with concrete details.
  • It shows what you did, not only what happened to you.
  • It connects your path to graduate study.
  • It says what you’ll contribute to the department.
  • It doesn’t repeat your statement of purpose.
  • You’re comfortable with everything you’ve disclosed.
  • It fits the length limit.

A last word

The best personal statements are specific and calm. They show a person with a clear path who made thoughtful choices along it, and who will make the department better by being there.

If yours does that in plain language, it has done its job. Good luck with your application.
